\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

Every algorithm below is implemented from scratch in Python; library code is used only for file
input/output, plotting, and for cross-checks in the test suite.

\subsection{Tokenisation}
\label{sec:tokenisation}

Each tweet is lowercased (case folding). URLs and @mentions are removed, including the forms in
which the SentiMix files store them with spaces between the pieces (\texttt{https // t co / ID},
\texttt{@ name \_ part}). The text is then split into words, where a word starts with a letter or
digit in any script and continues with letters, digits and combining marks, so Devanagari vowel
signs stay inside their word; emojis and punctuation separate words and hashtags keep their text.
Finally every run of three or more identical letters is shortened to two. No language
identification is done: English words are kept as they are.

\subsection{Soundex baseline}

Lecture Soundex \citep[Section~3.4]{manning2008iir} (1) keeps the first letter; (2) changes
A, E, I, O, U, H, W, Y to 0; (3) maps B, F, P, V$\to$1; C, G, J, K, Q, S, X, Z$\to$2; D, T$\to$3;
L$\to$4; M, N$\to$5; R$\to$6; (4) repeatedly removes one of each pair of identical consecutive
digits; and (5) removes all zeros, pads with trailing zeros and keeps four positions. For example
\textsc{herman}$\to$\textsc{h}06505$\to$H655 and \textsc{hermann}$\to$H655. We follow the lecture
literally: the first letter is not itself coded, so \textsc{pfister} gives P123.

\subsection{H-Key}
\label{sec:hkey}

H-Key applies the rules of Table~\ref{tab:hkey} in order to every word made only of the letters
a--z; other tokens (numbers, Devanagari) are left unchanged. Every rule can be switched off for
ablations.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{H-Key rules, in the order they are applied, with spelling pairs from the SentiMix training
tweets that each rule joins.}
\label{tab:hkey}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.19\linewidth}p{0.47\linewidth}p{0.26\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Rule & Action & Joins \\
\midrule
Lone \textit{c} & \textit{c}$\to$\textit{k} when not followed by \textit{h} or \textit{ch}
  (runs first, so \textit{ch} never becomes \textit{k}) & congress/kongress \\
De-aspirate & kh$\to$k, gh$\to$g, ch$\to$c, jh$\to$j, th$\to$t, dh$\to$d, ph$\to$f, bh$\to$b,
  sh$\to$s, repeated until none is left & thik/tik, phir/fir, achha/acha \\
Merge letters & w$\to$v, z$\to$j, q$\to$k, x$\to$ks & wala/vala, zindagi/jindagi, thanx/thanks \\
Squash & runs of the same consonant become one & pakka/paka, kutta/kuta \\
Vowels: V-drop & delete every vowel except a first letter & bahut/bohot/bahot \\
Vowels: V-norm & aa$\to$a; ee, ii$\to$i; oo, uu$\to$u; ai, ae$\to$e; au, ou$\to$o & pyaar/pyar,
  theek/thik, aur/our \\
Length cap & optional; keep the first $n$ letters (off by default) & doston/dosto ($n=3$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two choices keep the rules from destroying distinctions. The lone-\textit{c} rule runs before
de-aspiration, so \textit{ch} (\textit{chal}, ``walk'') stays apart from \textit{k}
(\textit{kal}, ``tomorrow''). Squashing touches only consonants, because V-norm must still see the
doubled vowels \textit{ee} and \textit{oo}. For example, \textit{achha} becomes \textit{aca} after
de-aspiration, so its V-drop key is \texttt{ac} and its V-norm key is \texttt{aca}; \textit{theek}
gets the V-drop key \texttt{tk} and the V-norm key \texttt{tik}.

\subsection{Learning variant classes}
\label{sec:variants}

Classes are learned from the word types of the training split only.

\paragraph{Blocking.} Words are grouped by their H-Key. Comparing every pair of the $V$ word types
would cost $O(V^2)$ comparisons; only pairs inside one key block are compared.

\paragraph{Similarity.} Each word is represented by the character trigrams of its
\emph{similarity form}, its H-Key under V-norm without a length cap, padded with \$ at both ends
(\texttt{\$fi}, \texttt{fir}, \texttt{ir\$} for \textit{phir}). Trigrams are weighted by tf-idf as
in L3, treating every word type as a document:
\begin{equation}
w_{g,a}=\bigl(1+\log_{10}\mathrm{tf}_{g,a}\bigr)\cdot\log_{10}\frac{N}{\mathrm{df}_g},
\qquad
\cos(\vec a,\vec b)=\frac{\vec a\cdot\vec b}{|\vec a|\,|\vec b|},
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of training word types and $\mathrm{df}_g$ the number containing trigram
$g$. Using the similarity form instead of the raw spelling lets the cosine see what the key already
knows: \textit{phir} and \textit{fir} share only one raw trigram but have identical forms.

\paragraph{Merging.} Two words of one block are merged when Equation~\ref{eq:merge} holds; merges
are closed transitively with a union--find structure (single-link), so every word ends up in exactly
one class. The class is named after its most frequent member. A word not seen in training joins the
class of its most similar same-key training word if that cosine reaches $\theta$, and otherwise
forms its own class.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Learning variant classes from the training word types}
\label{alg:variants}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require word types $W$ with frequencies, key function $\mathrm{key}$, threshold $\theta$
\State compute the unit tf-idf trigram vector $\vec v_w$ of the similarity form of each $w\in W$
\State $\mathit{blocks}\gets$ group $W$ by $\mathrm{key}(w)$
\State make a union--find set for every $w\in W$
\For{each block $B$}
  \For{each pair $a,b\in B$}
    \If{$\vec v_a\cdot\vec v_b\ge\theta$} \textsc{Union}$(a,b)$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\For{each set $S$} name every member of $S$ by $\arg\max_{w\in S}\mathrm{freq}(w)$ \EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Settings.} The vowel policy of the key and $\theta$ were chosen on the E1 development
groups (Section~\ref{sec:e1}): a V-drop key with $\theta=0.85$. With a V-norm key the threshold has no
effect, because every word of a block then has the same similarity form and every cosine is 1;
clustering on a V-norm key is therefore identical to plain V-norm H-Key.

\paragraph{$k$-means ablation.} As an alternative to the threshold rule, every block with at least
10 words is split by $k$-means \citep[Chapter~16]{manning2008iir} over the same vectors: $K$ seeds
are drawn at random (seed 42, best of 5 runs), and assignment to the nearest centroid alternates with
recomputing the centroids until no assignment changes. The residual sum of squares
$\mathrm{RSS}=\sum_{k}\sum_{\vec x\in\omega_k}|\vec x-\vec\mu(\omega_k)|^2$ is computed for
$K=1,\ldots,\lfloor|B|/2\rfloor$, and $K$ is taken at the knee of the RSS curve: the point furthest
below the straight line joining its first and last points (both axes scaled to $[0,1]$). Smaller
blocks keep the threshold rule.

\subsection{Positional inverted index}
\label{sec:index}

For every term $t$ the index stores a postings list of $(d, \langle p_1,p_2,\ldots\rangle)$ pairs,
the documents containing $t$ in increasing order, each with the sorted positions of $t$. Queries
are tokenised and normalised exactly like documents.

\paragraph{Boolean AND.} Two postings lists are intersected by advancing two pointers in step,
which costs $O(x+y)$ for lists of lengths $x$ and $y$ \citep[Figure~1.6]{manning2008iir}. For a
conjunction of several terms the terms are processed in increasing order of document frequency, so
every intermediate result is at most as long as the shortest list, and the merge stops as soon as
the result is empty.

\paragraph{Phrase queries.} A phrase $t_1t_2\ldots t_m$ is answered by positional intersection
\citep[Figure~2.12]{manning2008iir}. Starting from the postings of $t_1$, the postings of $t_i$ are
merged in, keeping in each shared document only the start positions $s$ for which $t_i$ occurs at
$s+i-1$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:posint}). Because the terms are equivalence classes, the phrase
\textit{bahut accha} also matches \textit{bohot acha} whenever the mode puts these spellings in the
same classes.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Positional intersection with an offset}
\label{alg:posint}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require postings $p_1,p_2$ of $(d,\text{positions})$ sorted by $d$; offset $o$
\State $\mathit{answer}\gets\langle\rangle$
\While{$p_1\neq\mathrm{nil}$ \textbf{and} $p_2\neq\mathrm{nil}$}
  \If{$\mathrm{doc}(p_1)=\mathrm{doc}(p_2)$}
    \State $\mathit{kept}\gets\{s\in\mathrm{pos}(p_1) : s+o\in\mathrm{pos}(p_2)\}$
      \Comment{two-pointer merge of the position lists}
    \If{$\mathit{kept}\neq\emptyset$} append $(\mathrm{doc}(p_1),\mathit{kept})$ to $\mathit{answer}$ \EndIf
    \State advance $p_1$ and $p_2$
  \ElsIf{$\mathrm{doc}(p_1)<\mathrm{doc}(p_2)$} advance $p_1$
  \Else\ advance $p_2$
  \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State \Return $\mathit{answer}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Ranked retrieval}
\label{sec:ranking}

Documents are ranked by cosine similarity with SMART lnc.ltc weighting
\citep[Section~6.4.3]{manning2008iir}. A document term gets
$w_{t,d}=(1+\log_{10}\mathrm{tf}_{t,d})$ and the document vector is length-normalised (lnc, no
idf); a query term gets $w_{t,q}=(1+\log_{10}\mathrm{tf}_{t,q})\cdot\log_{10}(N/\mathrm{df}_t)$ and
the query vector is length-normalised (ltc). The score is
\begin{equation}
\cos(\vec q,\vec d)=\frac{\vec q\cdot\vec d}{|\vec q|\,|\vec d|}
=\sum_{t\in q}\frac{w_{t,q}}{|\vec q|}\cdot\frac{w_{t,d}}{|\vec d|},
\end{equation}
accumulated term-at-a-time over the postings of the query terms, with the document lengths
$|\vec d|$ computed once when the index is loaded. The $K$ best of the $J$ documents with a non-zero
score are taken from a binary max-heap: building the heap bottom-up costs at most $2J$ comparisons
and each of the $K$ removals about $2\log J$, so $2J+2K\log J$ in total, which is less than sorting
all $J$ scores when $K\ll J$. Ties go to the earlier document. The implementation reproduces the
lnc.ltc worked example of \citet[Section~6.4.3]{manning2008iir} (score 0.8).

\subsection{Classifiers}
\label{sec:classifiers}

The normalised terms are the features of three classifiers (lectures L12--L13). Multinomial Naive
Bayes assigns
\begin{equation}
c_{\mathrm{map}}=\arg\max_{c}\Bigl[\log\hat P(c)+\sum_{1\le k\le n_d}\log\hat P(t_k\mid c)\Bigr],
\qquad
\hat P(t\mid c)=\frac{T_{ct}+1}{\sum_{t'}T_{ct'}+B},
\end{equation}
with add-one smoothing over the vocabulary size $B$, computed in log space. Rocchio represents each
class by its centroid $\vec\mu(c)=\frac{1}{|D_c|}\sum_{d\in D_c}\vec v(d)$ of tf-idf vectors and
assigns a document to the nearest centroid. $k$NN assigns the majority class among the $k$ training
documents with the highest cosine similarity. Section~\ref{sec:e2} gives the training protocol.

\subsection{Evaluation measures}
\label{sec:measures}

\paragraph{Variant grouping (E1).} A system's grouping is compared with the gold grouping by
counting \emph{pairs} of words placed in the same group. With $\mathit{TP}$ the pairs grouped by
both, precision $P=\mathit{TP}/|\text{system pairs}|$, recall $R=\mathit{TP}/|\text{gold pairs}|$ and
$F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}$; only words that occur in the gold set are scored.

\paragraph{Agreement.} Agreement between the two annotations is measured with Cohen's $\kappa$
\citep[Section~8.5]{manning2008iir}, $\kappa=\frac{P(A)-P(E)}{1-P(E)}$, where $P(A)$ is the observed
agreement and $P(E)=\sum_k p_A(k)\,p_B(k)$ is the agreement expected by chance from each annotator's
own label distribution.

\paragraph{Retrieval (E3).} For each query exactly one tweet is relevant. The reciprocal rank is
$1/r$ for the target at rank $r$ (0 if it is not retrieved); its mean over queries is the MRR.
P@1 is the fraction of queries whose target is ranked first, and R@$k$ the fraction with the target
in the top $k$. MAP is also reported: with a single relevant document the average precision of a
query equals its reciprocal rank, so MAP equals MRR here.

\subsection{Gold data}
\label{sec:gold}

\paragraph{Variant gold set (E1).} Candidate groups were drawn from the training vocabulary: 600
headwords sampled with seed 42 from three frequency bands, with 150 words tagged Hindi and 50 tagged
English (by the SentiMix word-level tags, used only for this sampling) in each band. Each headword
received up to six candidate spellings from four sources: edit distance at most 2, lecture Soundex,
H-Key with V-drop, and the V-norm similarity form. Two sources are independent of H-Key, so the gold
set does not contain only words that H-Key already groups, and the candidate file does not show
which source proposed a candidate. No word appears in two groups. Every candidate was labelled
against its headword as \textsc{same} (the same word spelled differently), \textsc{inflection} (the
same dictionary word in another grammatical form) or \textsc{different}, following a written guide.
Strict scores count only \textsc{same} pairs as true variants; lenient scores also count
\textsc{inflection}.

Both annotations were produced by language models, in separate sessions that saw only the labelling
guide and the candidate rows, with no access to the code, H-Key, any other system output or the
other annotation. Cohen's $\kappa$ between them is 0.960 over the three labels (observed agreement
0.981; 0.962 for strict and for lenient binary labels). This is \emph{agreement between two
independent model annotations, not human inter-annotator agreement}; since similar models are likely
to make correlated errors, it probably overstates how reliable the gold set is. The 69 disagreements
were adjudicated by a third language-model session that saw both labels in random order without
annotator identity; it sided with the second annotation 57 times and with the first 12 times. No
gold label was assigned by a human; one author reviewed 100 randomly chosen rows of the final gold
set and found no label to change. The final set has 3{,}541 labelled pairs in 600 groups:
776 \textsc{same}, 382 \textsc{inflection} and 2{,}383 \textsc{different}.

\paragraph{Retrieval queries (E3).} The 100 queries target 100 test tweets selected with seed 42
(rules in the data documentation). Each query has 3--6 words of its tweet, at least two of them Hindi
words respelled in a common alternative spelling. The queries were machine-generated by a language
model following these written rules, without access to H-Key or the index, and then reviewed and
edited by the authors; the file was frozen before the first retrieval run.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The variant classes and all classifier parameters are learned from the official training split
only. Every setting is chosen on development data, and test data is scored once with the chosen
settings. For E1 the 600 gold groups are split into 300 development and 300 test groups (seed 42);
the vowel policy of \texttt{hkey} and the vowel policy and $\theta\in\{0.20,0.25,\ldots,0.90\}$ of
\texttt{hkey\_clusters} are chosen by strict $F_1$ on the development groups. The decision to
report H-Key with V-drop as an extra ablation in E3 was taken before E3 was first run, and V-norm
stays the main H-Key mode regardless of the E3 scores. Every experiment is one command
(\texttt{experiments/e1\_variants.py}, \texttt{e3\_retrieval.py}, \texttt{e4\_efficiency.py}) and
records its configuration and results in a JSON file.
